The seed's journey

The first magnetic fields in the Universe were probably born in plasma structures far smaller than anything turbulence normally touches. Today we see magnetic fields stretched across whole clusters of galaxies. Can turbulent gas carry a tiny seed field up to those enormous sizes, in places from galaxy clusters to the emptiest cosmic voids? If it can, how, and how fast?

What exactly is plotted?

Left: the magnetic energy spectrum M(k,t) of the Kazantsev–Kraichnan model (in units of the initial energy per resistive wavenumber) against wavenumber k/kη, both on logarithmic axes. Wavenumber is inverse size, so small structures are on the right. The dashed line marks the resistive scale kη and the dotted lines the seed scales k0 = kη/10 and k0 = 10kη. Right: the total magnetic energy W(t)/W0. Time is in stretching times 1/γ̄: in one stretching time, the smallest eddies stretch a typical field line about 1.8-fold. Gold, solid: the seed born ten times larger than the resistive scale. Purple, dashed: the seed born ten times smaller. Both are narrow (all their energy near one size) and start with the same energy. Energies below 10−7 of the largest value in each frame are not drawn: that is the precision limit of the transform used.

Data: a numerical solution of the model's equation for the magnetic spectrum, computed with the project's checked and reviewed solver and stored as 248 frames. Between stored frames the curves are interpolated linearly in log energy. This is an idealized model (a random flow with no memory). Times shorter than a few eddy times have no counterpart in real turbulence. The project's simulations will test it.

Where do these numbers come from?

They are the model's own formulas, evaluated live in your browser. The slider sets x0 = k0/kη, the seed's wavenumber in units of the resistive one, from 0.1 to 100. The surviving fraction is the share of the seed's energy that ends up in the growing pattern: Fδ = Γ(5/4)2(2π)−1/2 x0−5/2 K0(x0), where K0 is a modified Bessel function. The recovery time is how long the growing pattern takes to bring the field back to its starting energy. The in-browser values were checked against an independent high-precision calculation. These are late-time formulas, approximate below about x0 = 5 (hence "about" in the readouts), and they are shown only from x0 = 3 up.

For smaller x0 the page says in words what happens. A narrow seed with x0 below about 1.7 never loses energy: stretching beats resistance from the start. Between about 1.7 and 3 its energy dips for a few stretching times and then grows. The 1.7 comes from a full numerical solution of the model, checked independently.

The formulas stop being trustworthy for real turbulence at large x0: the model assumes a flow with no memory, and for real eddies that assumption fails beyond about x0 = 10–30. Up there the number is an extrapolation of the idealized model, which our simulations will test. The model also needs the seed to be much smaller than the smallest eddy; in the animation above the larger seed is still 100 times smaller than the eddies.

A way to magnetize the Universe from any seed

Turbulence pervades cosmic gas. It captures any magnetic seed, however tiny and however it was born, amplifies it, and carries the field up to large scales.

In more detail: no seed is too small-scale. The turbulent dynamo captures any finite seed field, at an exponential cost in amplitude for narrow seeds born below the resistive scale, and established nonlinear-dynamo results then carry the field to the outer scale.

Two sources stand behind that sentence. The capture and its cost are what the model on this page shows: a narrow seed born above the resistive scale loses nothing, and one born below it keeps only an exponentially small share. The last step, growth from the resistive scale up to the size of the stirring, comes from published dynamo simulations (Beattie et al. 2022; Kriel et al. 2025), which started from seeds at or above the resistive scale.

Capture is shown in our model; growth to large scales is established in dynamo simulations (Beattie et al. 2022; Kriel et al. 2025); our simulations with the AthenaK code will test the full chain for seeds born below the resistive scale. No simulation results are shown here yet.

How this was made

Steps 2 to 4 use one classic theory of the turbulent dynamo, the Kazantsev–Kraichnan model of a kinematic dynamo: a weak magnetic field stretched and folded by a random, fast-changing flow, before the field is strong enough to push back. The animation, the 3D landscape and the numbers in the readouts come from the project's own solutions of that model, which were checked independently and reviewed before they appear here. The slider's formulas are computed live in your browser. Nothing is painted by hand. The scale sizes in step 1 come from published estimates.

The model is an idealization, and the page says where it stops being trustworthy. This page is part of a research project on how turbulence could magnetize the Universe from any seed, however small. The project's simulations will test this idea.
