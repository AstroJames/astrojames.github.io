The seed's journey

The first magnetic fields in the Universe were probably born in plasma structures far smaller than anything turbulence normally touches. Today we see magnetic fields stretched across whole clusters of galaxies. Can turbulent gas carry a tiny seed field up to those enormous sizes, in places from galaxy clusters to the emptiest cosmic voids? If it can, how, and how fast?

1 About sixteen powers of ten

Each rung is a size that matters in a galaxy cluster, from where the seed is born to where fields are seen. Click a rung, or focus the ruler and use the ← → keys. Small numbers point to the sources listed at the bottom of the page.

Sizes are orders of magnitude for the hot gas of a typical galaxy cluster: about one particle per thousand cubic centimetres, at about 60 million degrees, stirred by eddies a few hundred thousand light-years across4,5. They follow from standard plasma formulas and the published estimates cited on each rung. Everyday comparisons are rounded. Whether the seed really starts below the resistive scale is itself uncertain: it depends on what sets resistance in such a thin, hot plasma, which is still debated3,6.

2 How turbulence stirs

The gas in a cluster is turbulent. Large eddies, stirred at the top of the ladder, break up into smaller and smaller ones, handing their energy down a cascade until the gas's internal friction (viscosity) turns it into heat. Smaller eddies spin faster, so the smallest ones stretch a magnetic field line the fastest. Choose a stage to see where it sits.

What is real here, and what is illustrative?

Real: the energy curve follows Kolmogorov's 1941 law, energy per wavenumber ∝ k−5/3, as stated in the review of ref.6 (eq. 2.1). The same law makes an eddy of size ℓ turn over at a rate ∝ ℓ−2/3: that sets the spin of the drawn eddies, the lengths of the bars and the speed of the orange energy packet. The magnetic curve has the shape of the growing pattern of the dynamo model used in steps 4 to 6, x3/2K0(x) with x = k/kη, which peaks at the resistive scale; simulations find the field concentrated there too8.

Illustrative: the drawing of the eddies, the number of steps (five here), the exact shape of the drop at the viscous scale, the height of the magnetic curve, and the spacing of the marked sizes. In a real cluster the gaps are far larger (step 1), and where the viscous scale sits in such a thin, hot plasma is uncertain4,5. The rungs of step 1 map onto this picture as follows: the largest eddies are the top rung, the resistive scale is the rung near the bottom, and the seed is born below it, at the electron skin depth.

3 Stretch, twist, fold

In hot cluster gas, a magnetic field line is carried along by the flow like a thread of dye stirred into it, and stretching the line makes the field along it stronger9. Here a closed loop of field is stirred by eddies of four sizes, each moving with the speed Kolmogorov's law gives it. Press Play, then drag to turn the box.

Eddies stirring the loop (changing this restarts the run)

Try the largest eddies alone, then all four sizes. The earlier runs stay on the chart, faded. With small eddies stirring, the loop grows far faster, because the smallest eddies set the pace7. The folds also get thinner and thinner, packing the field into ever smaller sizes. In real gas, resistance (left out here) stops the folding near the resistive scale. Without resistance, the loop's energy grows at the rate the dynamo model of the next step predicts for that case, about sevenfold per stretching time. Once resistance acts, as in step 4, the growth is slower.

How is this computed?

The loop is a chain of points moved by the flow, which is exact for a field line in a perfectly conducting gas. Where the line has been stretched, the field is stronger by the same factor (colour: CMasher ember, brighter is stronger, logarithmic from 0.3 to 3,000 times the starting field). The magnetic energy of a thin loop of fixed magnetic flux grows as the sum of the squared stretching factors. Points are added wherever the line gets coarse, up to 160,000.

The flow is a stand-in for turbulence: each eddy size is a single random wave of shearing motion that is replaced by a new random one after one turnover time. Its speed and lifetime follow Kolmogorov's law, so an eddy of half the size moves 0.79 times as fast and turns over 1.6 times as often. The equations of motion are solved exactly in your browser. A flow that changes this quickly is the kind assumed by the Kazantsev model of step 410. Time is in that model's "stretching times", and the dashed lines are the model's growth rates: the energy grows at twice the stretching rate and the length at 0.8 times. With all four eddy sizes the computed run follows them to within about 10%, and an independent check confirms this. With only the largest eddies, the run lasts less than one of their own stretching times, too short to settle onto the rate, so it stays below the line.

Illustrative choices: only four eddy sizes; single-wave eddies instead of real turbulence; no resistance; the loop's size and starting place; the box repeats, so a line leaving one face comes back through the opposite face. The view is not a simulation of a galaxy cluster.

5 A race against resistance, drawn as a landscape

How can a seed born too small be amplified at all? In the model it is a race against resistance. At the size where such a seed is born, resistance is erasing it. The turbulence's random stretching and shearing makes the seed's size wander, sometimes down and sometimes up. Most of the seed is erased. A small share is carried up to the resistive scale before resistance erases it, and that share is what the dynamo captures. The smaller the seed is born, the further that share has to travel, and the less of it arrives.

In our model, turbulence makes a seed's size wander while resistance erases it, and only the share that is carried up to about the resistive scale, where growth can win, before it is erased gets to grow. Choose the seed's size and press Play.

What am I looking at?

This is our model of the turbulent dynamo, written in a form that looks like the equation for a quantum particle in a landscape, and run so that lower means growth. The wall is resistance, and the flat valley is where turbulence makes the field grow. Nothing tunnels through the wall. Nothing rolls down it either: the wall's height is how fast resistance erases, not a slope that pushes. The turbulence does the carrying: it moves the seed's energy from each size to the neighbouring sizes, while resistance erases it fastest where the wall is highest.

The growing pattern keeps most of its energy at sizes a little smaller than the resistive scale (here it peaks about 2 times smaller), because turbulence keeps pushing energy towards smaller sizes, where resistance removes it. It still grows, because stretching adds energy faster than resistance removes it. Counted as in step 4, the same pattern peaks at the resistive scale. This is a picture of the model: our simulations do not show whether the capture happens by this race.

The seed here is narrower than the smaller seed in step 4, so its energy falls a little lower here than there.

6 Move the seed

Now you choose where a narrow seed is born, from ten times larger than the resistive scale to a hundred times smaller. Drag the slider and see how much of the seed survives to be amplified, according to the model.

What the model says happens to this seed

This curve is the model's estimate, not a measurement. In the range our first simulations tested (seeds up to about 4 times smaller than the resistive scale), the simulated seeds kept more energy than our model predicted: 3 to 6 times more, relative to a reference seed born at the resistive scale (see the first test below). In that range the model is too pessimistic. The test compared each smaller seed with the reference seed over a set time window. That is related to this curve but is not the same quantity, so the “3 to 6 times” cannot be applied to the numbers shown here (see the details below). Seeds more than about 4 times smaller are untested. Beyond about 10–30 times smaller, the number is an extrapolation of the idealized model, and real turbulence may differ.

Where do these numbers come from?

They are the model's own formulas, evaluated live in your browser. The slider sets x0 = k0/kη, the seed's wavenumber in units of the resistive one, from 0.1 to 100. The surviving fraction is the share of the seed's energy that ends up in the growing pattern: Fδ = Γ(5/4)2(2π)−1/2 x0−5/2 K0(x0), where K0 is a modified Bessel function. The recovery time is how long the growing pattern takes to bring the field back to its starting energy. The in-browser values were checked against an independent high-precision calculation. These are late-time formulas, approximate below about x0 = 5 (hence "about" in the readouts), and they are shown only from x0 = 3 up.

Our first simulations did not measure this curve directly. They tested the same model, solved in time for those runs, through a related quantity: the energy of each smaller seed divided by the energy of a reference seed born at the resistive scale, averaged from 2 to 5 turnover times after the seeds were added. The "3 to 6 times" above refers to that comparison.

For smaller x0 the page says in words what happens in the model. A narrow seed with x0 below about 1.7 never loses energy: stretching beats resistance from the start. Between about 1.7 and 3 its energy dips for a few stretching times and then grows. The 1.7 comes from a full numerical solution of the model, checked independently.

The formulas are least trustworthy for real turbulence at large x0: the model assumes a flow with no memory, and for real eddies that assumption fails beyond about x0 = 10–3011,8. Up there the number is an extrapolation of the idealized model. Our first simulations reached seeds about 4 times smaller than the resistive scale, so they do not test that range. In the range they did test, the model already predicts too large a loss. So neither the model nor the simulations tell us yet what happens to the far smaller seeds of a real galaxy cluster. The model also needs the seed to be much smaller than the smallest eddy; in the animation above the larger seed is still 100 times smaller than the eddies.

The first test: what our simulations show

Everything in steps 4 to 6 is a model, so we tested it. We ran computer simulations of turbulent, electrically conducting gas and added very weak magnetic seeds of four sizes: a reference seed born at the resistive scale, and seeds about 2, 3 and 4 times smaller than it. The model's predictions were written down before the test.

Every seed we tested was captured. Within a few turnover times (a turnover time is the time the largest eddies take to turn over once), each smaller seed took on the same growing pattern as the reference seed in the same flow: its energy was spread across sizes in the same way. That is what we call capture. Capture does not mean that a seed has regained its starting energy: the smallest seed had not when the runs ended.

The seeds kept more energy than the model predicted. As in the model, a smaller seed kept less. But relative to the reference seed, the smaller seeds kept 3 to 6 times more energy than our model predicted (averaged from 2 to 5 turnover times after the seeds were added): 3 times more for the seed about 2 times smaller, and 6 times more for the seed about 4 times smaller. The model's prediction, written down before the test, fails that test: it predicts too large a loss.

What is not yet known. These are two repeats at one resolution. Seeds more than about 4 times smaller than the resistive scale are untested, so neither the model nor the simulations tell us yet what happens to the far smaller seeds of a real galaxy cluster. Why the seeds do better than the model predicted is not yet tested, and the simulations do not show whether the capture happens by the race described in step 5. The simulated seeds are weak magnetic fields placed in a conducting fluid, at and below its resistive scale. The simulations do not include the particle-scale physics that would create a real seed.

A way to magnetize the Universe from any seed

Turbulence pervades cosmic gas. It captures any magnetic seed, however tiny and however it was born, amplifies it, and carries the field up to large scales.

In more detail, no seed is too small-scale: in our model, the turbulent dynamo captures any finite seed field, at an exponential cost in amplitude for narrow seeds born below the resistive scale. Our first simulations show this capture for every seed tested, at a lower cost than the model predicts. Established nonlinear-dynamo results then carry the field to the outer scale.

Three sources stand behind that statement. The capture and its exponential cost are what the model on this page shows: in the model, a narrow seed born above the resistive scale loses nothing, and one born below it keeps only an exponentially small share. The capture at a lower cost is what our first simulations show, for seeds up to about 4 times smaller than the resistive scale and no smaller (the first test, above). The last step, growth from the resistive scale up to the size of the stirring, comes from published dynamo simulations12,13, which started from seeds at or above the resistive scale.

Capture is shown in our model; growth to large scales is established in dynamo simulations12,13. Our first simulations, run with the AthenaK code, show capture for seeds born up to about 4 times smaller than the resistive scale (two repeats at one resolution). Smaller seeds are not yet tested. The model's prediction, written down before the test, fails it: relative to the reference seed, the smaller seeds keep 3 to 6 times more energy than it predicted. The later strong-field (nonlinear) stage, which carries the field to large scales, is shown by those published simulations and is outside this project. The animations and the slider on this page show the model. The simulation results are given in words.

How this was made

Steps 4 to 6 use one classic theory of the turbulent dynamo, the Kazantsev–Kraichnan model of a kinematic dynamo: a weak magnetic field stretched and folded by a random, fast-changing flow, before the field is strong enough to push back. The animation and the numbers in the readouts come from the project's own solutions of that model, which were checked independently and reviewed before they appear here. The slider's formulas, the cascade's scalings and the 3D loop's motion are computed live in your browser. Nothing is painted by hand, and the parts that are illustrative say so. The scale sizes in step 1 come from published estimates.

The model is an idealization. The page says where it has been tested, where its numbers fail and where it is only an extrapolation. This page is part of a research project on how turbulence could magnetize the Universe from any seed, however small. The project's first simulations have tested the first step, the capture, for seeds up to about 4 times smaller than the resistive scale. Their results, given in words above, passed the project's own internal science review before they appeared here. They are first results: they have not been peer-reviewed by independent scientists or published in a journal.
