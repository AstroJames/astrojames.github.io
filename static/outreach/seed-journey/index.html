The seed's journey

The first magnetic fields in the Universe were probably born in plasma structures far smaller than anything turbulence normally touches. Today we see magnetic fields stretched across whole clusters of galaxies. Can turbulent gas carry a tiny seed field up to those enormous sizes, in places from galaxy clusters to the emptiest cosmic voids? If it can, how, and how fast?

1 About sixteen powers of ten

Each rung is a size that matters in a galaxy cluster, from where the seed is born to where fields are seen. Click a rung, or focus the ruler and use the ← → keys. Small numbers point to the sources listed at the bottom of the page.

Sizes are orders of magnitude for the hot gas of a typical galaxy cluster: about one particle per thousand cubic centimetres, at about 60 million degrees, stirred by eddies a few hundred thousand light-years across4,5. They follow from standard plasma formulas and the published estimates cited on each rung. Everyday comparisons are rounded. Whether the seed really starts below the resistive scale is itself uncertain: it depends on what sets resistance in such a thin, hot plasma, which is still debated3,6.

2 How turbulence stirs

The gas in a cluster is turbulent. Large eddies, stirred at the top of the ladder, break up into smaller and smaller ones, handing their energy down a cascade until the gas's internal friction (viscosity) turns it into heat. Smaller eddies spin faster, so the smallest ones stretch a magnetic field line the fastest. Choose a stage to see where it sits.

What is real here, and what is illustrative?

Real: the energy curve follows Kolmogorov's 1941 law, energy per wavenumber ∝ k−5/3, as stated in the review of ref.6 (eq. 2.1). The same law makes an eddy of size ℓ turn over at a rate ∝ ℓ−2/3: that sets the spin of the drawn eddies, the lengths of the bars and the speed of the orange energy packet. The magnetic curve has the shape of the growing pattern of the dynamo model used in steps 4 and 5, x3/2K0(x) with x = k/kη, which peaks at the resistive scale; simulations find the field concentrated there too8.

Illustrative: the drawing of the eddies, the number of steps (five here), the exact shape of the drop at the viscous scale, the height of the magnetic curve, and the spacing of the marked sizes. In a real cluster the gaps are far larger (step 1), and where the viscous scale sits in such a thin, hot plasma is uncertain4,5. The rungs of step 1 map onto this picture as follows: the largest eddies are the top rung, the resistive scale is the rung near the bottom, and the seed is born below it, at the electron skin depth.

3 Stretch, twist, fold

In hot cluster gas, a magnetic field line is carried along by the flow like a thread of dye stirred into it, and stretching the line makes the field along it stronger9. Here a closed loop of field is stirred by eddies of four sizes, each moving with the speed Kolmogorov's law gives it. Press Play, then drag to turn the box.

Eddies stirring the loop (changing this restarts the run)

Try the largest eddies alone, then all four sizes. The earlier runs stay on the chart, faded. With small eddies stirring, the loop grows far faster, because the smallest eddies set the pace7. The folds also get thinner and thinner, packing the field into ever smaller sizes. In real gas, resistance (left out here) stops the folding near the resistive scale. Without resistance, the loop's energy grows at the rate the dynamo model of the next step predicts for that case, about sevenfold per stretching time. Once resistance acts, as in step 4, the growth is slower.

How is this computed?

The loop is a chain of points moved by the flow, which is exact for a field line in a perfectly conducting gas. Where the line has been stretched, the field is stronger by the same factor (colour: CMasher ember, brighter is stronger, logarithmic from 0.3 to 3,000 times the starting field). The magnetic energy of a thin loop of fixed magnetic flux grows as the sum of the squared stretching factors. Points are added wherever the line gets coarse, up to 160,000.

The flow is a stand-in for turbulence: each eddy size is a single random wave of shearing motion that is replaced by a new random one after one turnover time. Its speed and lifetime follow Kolmogorov's law, so an eddy of half the size moves 0.79 times as fast and turns over 1.6 times as often. The equations of motion are solved exactly in your browser. A flow that changes this quickly is the kind assumed by the Kazantsev model of step 410. Time is in that model's "stretching times", and the dashed lines are the model's growth rates: the energy grows at twice the stretching rate and the length at 0.8 times. With all four eddy sizes the computed run follows them to within about 10%, and an independent check confirms this. With only the largest eddies, the run lasts less than one of their own stretching times, too short to settle onto the rate, so it stays below the line.

Illustrative choices: only four eddy sizes; single-wave eddies instead of real turbulence; no resistance; the loop's size and starting place; the box repeats, so a line leaving one face comes back through the opposite face. The view is not a simulation of a galaxy cluster.

What exactly is plotted?

Left: the magnetic energy spectrum M(k,t) of the Kazantsev–Kraichnan model (in units of the initial energy per resistive wavenumber) against wavenumber k/kη, both on logarithmic axes. Wavenumber is inverse size, so small structures are on the right. The dashed line marks the resistive scale kη and the dotted lines the seed scales k0 = kη/10 and k0 = 10kη. Right: the total magnetic energy W(t)/W0. Time is in stretching times 1/γ̄: in one stretching time, the smallest eddies stretch a typical field line about 1.8-fold. Gold, solid: the seed born ten times larger than the resistive scale. Purple, dashed: the seed born ten times smaller. Both are narrow (all their energy near one size) and start with the same energy. Energies below 10−7 of the largest value in each frame are not drawn: that is the precision limit of the transform used.

Data: a numerical solution of the model's equation for the magnetic spectrum, computed with the project's checked and reviewed solver and stored as 248 frames. Between stored frames the curves are interpolated linearly in log energy. This is an idealized model (a random flow with no memory). Times shorter than a few eddy times have no counterpart in real turbulence. The project's simulations will test it.

5 Move the seed

Now you choose where a narrow seed is born, from ten times larger than the resistive scale to a hundred times smaller. Drag the slider and see how much of the seed survives to be amplified.

What happens to this seed

Beyond about 10–30 times smaller, the number is an extrapolation of the idealized model. Real turbulence may differ, and our simulations will test it.

Where do these numbers come from?

They are the model's own formulas, evaluated live in your browser. The slider sets x0 = k0/kη, the seed's wavenumber in units of the resistive one, from 0.1 to 100. The surviving fraction is the share of the seed's energy that ends up in the growing pattern: Fδ = Γ(5/4)2(2π)−1/2 x0−5/2 K0(x0), where K0 is a modified Bessel function. The recovery time is how long the growing pattern takes to bring the field back to its starting energy. The in-browser values were checked against an independent high-precision calculation. These are late-time formulas, approximate below about x0 = 5 (hence "about" in the readouts), and they are shown only from x0 = 3 up.

For smaller x0 the page says in words what happens. A narrow seed with x0 below about 1.7 never loses energy: stretching beats resistance from the start. Between about 1.7 and 3 its energy dips for a few stretching times and then grows. The 1.7 comes from a full numerical solution of the model, checked independently.

The formulas stop being trustworthy for real turbulence at large x0: the model assumes a flow with no memory, and for real eddies that assumption fails beyond about x0 = 10–3011,8. Up there the number is an extrapolation of the idealized model, which our simulations will test. The model also needs the seed to be much smaller than the smallest eddy; in the animation above the larger seed is still 100 times smaller than the eddies.

A way to magnetize the Universe from any seed

Turbulence pervades cosmic gas. It captures any magnetic seed, however tiny and however it was born, amplifies it, and carries the field up to large scales.

In more detail: no seed is too small-scale. The turbulent dynamo captures any finite seed field, at an exponential cost in amplitude for narrow seeds born below the resistive scale, and established nonlinear-dynamo results then carry the field to the outer scale.

Two sources stand behind that sentence. The capture and its cost are what the model on this page shows: a narrow seed born above the resistive scale loses nothing, and one born below it keeps only an exponentially small share. The last step, growth from the resistive scale up to the size of the stirring, comes from published dynamo simulations12,13, which started from seeds at or above the resistive scale.

Capture is shown in our model; growth to large scales is established in dynamo simulations12,13; our simulations with the AthenaK code will test the full chain for seeds born below the resistive scale. No simulation results are shown here yet.

How this was made

Steps 4 and 5 use one classic theory of the turbulent dynamo, the Kazantsev–Kraichnan model of a kinematic dynamo: a weak magnetic field stretched and folded by a random, fast-changing flow, before the field is strong enough to push back. The animation and the numbers in the readouts come from the project's own solutions of that model, which were checked independently and reviewed before they appear here. The slider's formulas, the cascade's scalings and the 3D loop's motion are computed live in your browser. Nothing is painted by hand, and the parts that are illustrative say so. The scale sizes in step 1 come from published estimates.

The model is an idealization, and the page says where it stops being trustworthy. This page is part of a research project on how turbulence could magnetize the Universe from any seed, however small. The project's simulations will test this idea.
